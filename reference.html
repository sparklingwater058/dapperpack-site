Structural reference

Every box is a flat sheet that has been told what to do.

This page is the reference we work from: what each structure is for, how the drawing is laid out, and the trade terms that appear on a dieline. Nothing here is a client project — these are our own structural drawings, published so you can judge the work before you send anything.

Judge us on the dieline, the sample and a live walk of the workshop.

Reading the drawing

Four line types carry the whole specification

A dieline is read by convention. Once you know these four, you can check any drawing we send — including whether the grain runs the way the box needs it to.

Cut line — black, solid
The blade cuts here; it becomes the outer edge of the box. Artwork must bleed 3 mm past it, which is why every drawing below carries a green dashed bleed line outside the outline.
Fold line — red, dashed
A crease, not a cut. Never run text or a logo across it — the panel folds and the artwork breaks in two.
Glue flap
Glued under another panel and never seen. Leave it unprinted, or a flat colour. Its width depends on board thickness, and it is the first thing to fail if drawn too thin.
Tuck-in
Folds inside the box. Only put artwork here if you want it seen when the box is opened.
Panel labels
Our drawings name every panel — front, side, back, glue flap, tuck-in — and the labels are set the way the panel will finally face. On a folded box some of them read upside down. Follow the label, not the upright.

If a structure is still unclear after reading, print the drawing small, cut it out and fold it. For a complex box that five-minute step saves a reprint.

Structures

What we draw, and what each one is for

Five structures cover most briefs. Each one is drawn flat, because that is where the differences live — a tuck end and a crash-lock base look identical from the outside, and nothing alike on the sheet.

01

Straight tuck end

The everyday retail carton. Top and bottom tuck the same way, so one pass closes both ends.

  • one piece
  • glued seam
  • dust flaps
02

Reverse tuck end

Same strip, opposite bottom tuck. Pick it when the packed product has to slide in from one side only.

  • one piece
  • opens either end
  • glued seam
03

Crash-lock bottom

The bottom locks itself as the box is erected. Stronger than a tuck end and faster to pack on a line.

  • interlocking base
  • one movement
  • e-commerce
04

Book-style box

Cover, spine and back cover in one piece; the lid hinges along the spine and closes on magnets.

  • magnet closure
  • rigid
  • gift set
05

Drawer box

Two pieces: a printed sleeve and a tray that slides inside it. The fit between them is set at the drawing stage.

  • sleeve + tray
  • ribbon pull
  • rigid

Materials and finishing

The vocabulary a buyer uses on the phone

These are the terms we use in a quote, in the same words. If a supplier answers these loosely, the sample will show it.

Greyboard
The rigid core of a set-up box, measured in millimetres of thickness and quoted by grammage. The wrap is what you see; the greyboard is what you feel.
Wrap
The printed paper laminated over greyboard. Wrap direction decides which way the print runs across the lid, and where the turn-in lands on the inside.
Crease
A pressed fold line, as distinct from a cut. Scoring and creasing are the same operation — both press a rule into the board so it folds. A crease that is too shallow lets the printed surface crack along the fold; too deep and the board splits.
Fitment
The insert that locates the product. Cut from card or E-flute to the outline of the item, or built as a platform with wells.
Bleed
Print that runs past the cut line so no white edge appears after trimming. We set the bleed on the drawing; your artwork needs to cover it.
Spot colour
A single mixed ink printed as its own plate, matched to a reference you supply, instead of built from process inks.
