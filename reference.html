Structural reference

Every box is a flat sheet that has been told what to do.

This page is the reference we work from: what each structure is for, how the drawing is laid out, and the trade terms that appear on a dieline. Nothing here is a client project — these are our own structural drawings, published so you can judge the work before you send anything.

Judge us on the dieline, the sample and a live walk of the workshop.

Reading the drawing

Four line types carry the whole specification

A dieline is read by convention. Once you know these four, you can check any drawing we send — including whether the grain runs the way the box needs it to.

Cut line — black, solid
The blade cuts here; it becomes the outer edge of the box. Artwork must bleed 3 mm past it, which is why every drawing below carries a green dashed bleed line outside the outline.
Fold line — red, dashed
A crease, not a cut. Never run text or a logo across it — the panel folds and the artwork breaks in two.
Glue flap
Glued under another panel and never seen, so leave it unprinted or a flat colour. Its width follows board thickness, and it is the first thing to fail if drawn too thin.
Tuck-in
Folds inside the box. Only put artwork here if you want it seen when the box is opened.
Panel labels
Our drawings name every panel, and the labels are set the way the panel will finally face — on a folded box some of them read upside down. Follow the label, not the upright.

If a structure is still unclear after reading, print the drawing small, cut it out and fold it. For a complex box that five-minute step saves a reprint. Every structure below is drawn twice: the flat sheet, and the same box made up as a white sample with our own mark, out in 7 days.

Structures

What we draw, and what each one is for

Five structures cover most briefs. Each one is drawn flat, because that is where the differences live — a tuck end and a crash-lock base look identical from the outside, and nothing alike on the sheet.

Slotted card box

One-piece card blank, grooved and laminated. The round-cornered side panels carry a long slot that holds the fitting, and the lid and base insert tab are cut on the same blank.

Corrugated tuck-end carton

E-flute carton with dust flaps and a glue flap. The tuck ends close by hand or on a line, so neither end needs glue.

Drawer box

One drawer box takes two knives: the outer-box greyboard, cut flat, and the printed wrap that is glued over it. The wrap folds its four flaps on the creases shown.

  • two knives
  • rigid
04

Lid and base box

Product paper for one set, shown flat: the greyboard that gives the box its shape, and the printed wrap that is turned in over it. Each is cut on its own knife, and the wrap's corners fold on the creases shown.

  • greyboard + wrap
  • two knives
  • rigid
05

Book-style box

Four knives for one product: the inner-box wrap and the greyboard it covers, then the case wrap and the greyboard that becomes the book cover. The case greyboard is drawn as one unit of a two-up imposition; its two vertical lines are the spine.

  • book cover

Which tuck end?

The carton above is a tuck end. If the job is a folding carton, the choice that changes the tool is how the base closes and which way the tucks face — the three options below are the ones we cut most.

Comparison of straight tuck, reverse tuck and crash-lock folding cartons
  Straight tuck end Reverse tuck end Crash-lock bottom
Tuck direction Top and bottom tuck the same way Bottom tuck faces the opposite way Top tucks; base interlocks
How it closes Hand or line, one end then the other Either end first — useful when the product only loads one way The base locks itself as the box is erected
Base strength Standard Standard Stronger — the load sits on interlocked panels, not on a tuck
Packing speed Two ends to tuck Two ends to tuck Fastest to pack — the base is already locked
Board Paperboard Paperboard Paperboard, or E-flute when it needs to carry weight
Good for Retail shelf, cosmetics, small electronics Bottles and jars, where the product slides in from one end E-commerce and heavier contents

Materials and finishing

The vocabulary a buyer uses on the phone

These are the terms we use in a quote, in the same words. If a supplier answers these loosely, the sample will show it.

Greyboard
The rigid core of a set-up box, measured in millimetres of thickness and quoted by grammage. The wrap is what you see; the greyboard is what you feel.
Wrap
The printed paper laminated over greyboard. Wrap direction decides which way the print runs across the lid, and where the turn-in lands on the inside.
Crease
A pressed fold line, as distinct from a cut. Scoring and creasing are the same operation — both press a rule into the board so it folds. A crease that is too shallow lets the printed surface crack along the fold; too deep and the board splits.
Fitment
The insert that locates the product. Cut from card or E-flute to the outline of the item, or built as a platform with wells.
Bleed
Print that runs past the cut line so no white edge appears after trimming. We set the bleed on the drawing; your artwork needs to cover it.
Spot colour
A single mixed ink printed as its own plate, matched to a reference you supply, instead of built from process inks.
